SAT Reading and Writing questions use short passages or passage pairs. Each passage has only one question, so you do not need to memorize a long passage or answer several questions about it. Your job is to identify the question type, read for the specific information that type requires, and choose the answer that is most precisely supported.

You do **not** need to understand every unfamiliar scientific or historical detail. The passage supplies the information you need. Focus on the author's claim, the logical relationship between ideas, or the grammar structure being tested.

## A Process for Every Reading Question

:::strategy
1. **Read the question stem first.** Know whether you need a central idea, inference, word meaning, piece of evidence, or the function of a sentence.
2. **Read the passage and identify its job.** Ask what the author is trying to establish.
3. **Predict the answer before reading the choices.** The prediction can be a short paraphrase.
4. **Demand textual proof.** Do not select an answer merely because it sounds reasonable or is possible outside the passage.
5. **Identify the flaw in each remaining answer.** Name why it fails instead of relying only on instinct.
:::

Common wrong-answer flaws include:

- too strong or too broad,
- reverses a relationship,
- mentions something the passage never discusses,
- is true but does not answer the question,
- matches one detail but misses the passage's conclusion.

:::key{name="Reading rule"}
The correct answer must be supported. It does not merely need to be possible.
:::

Words such as *always*, *never*, *proves*, *entirely*, *only*, *all*, *impossible*, and *most likely* require strong evidence. Be suspicious of that level of certainty unless the passage clearly supports it.

### Read for a Job, Not for Every Detail

Before reading deeply, translate the stem into a simple task:

- **Central idea:** “What is the passage mainly saying?”
- **Detail:** “What does the passage directly state?”
- **Inference:** “What must or most reasonably follows?”
- **Function:** “Why did the author include this part?”
- **Transition:** “How are these two ideas related?”
- **Grammar:** “What sentence structure is legal here?”

This prevents a common problem: understanding the passage but answering a different question from the one asked.

<div class="theorem-box">

**Example.** A student understands that a passage discusses a successful conservation project, but the question asks for the function of the final sentence. What should the student determine before reading the answer choices?

The student should not merely repeat that the project was successful. The task word *function* asks why the final sentence is present. The student should decide whether it gives evidence, presents a result, qualifies the success, or performs another role in the passage.

</div>

### Eliminate with a Specific Reason

When two answers seem possible, return to the passage and name the difference between them. Ask:

- Which choice needs an extra assumption?
- Which choice changes *some* into *all* or *may* into *will*?
- Which choice answers the exact stem?
- Can I point to a word, sentence, or grammar rule that proves one choice?

:::tip
Do not ask which answer sounds better. Ask which answer you can defend with less explanation. The correct choice usually follows directly from the text or rule; the trap usually needs you to add something.
:::

## Pacing

Do not force every Reading and Writing question into the same time limit. Grammar questions may take less time than difficult inference, graph, or paired-text questions.

As a rough guide:

- straightforward grammar: 25–45 seconds,
- vocabulary and transitions: 40–60 seconds,
- reading and inference: 60–90 seconds,
- difficult graph or paired-text questions: up to about 90 seconds.

After roughly 90 seconds, eliminate what you can, choose the best-supported answer, mark it for review, and move on.

:::summary{title="One sentence to remember"}
Reading questions require proof; grammar questions require structure.
:::
