This domain focuses on vocabulary in context, the purpose and organization of a passage, and relationships between related texts.

## Words in Context

The SAT asks for the meaning of a word in its sentence, not necessarily its most common dictionary definition.

:::strategy{title="Solving words-in-context questions"}
1. Cover the choices.
2. Replace the tested word with a simple word of your own.
3. Determine whether the sentence's tone is positive, negative, or neutral.
4. Choose the answer that matches both the meaning and tone.
:::

For example, in “The scientist's findings qualified the earlier theory,” *qualified* most likely means *limited* or *modified*, not *made eligible*.

Common traps include:

- a familiar definition that does not fit the context,
- approximately the right tone but the wrong meaning,
- a word that is too extreme,
- two similar words when only one is precise.

## Text Structure and Purpose

Do not ask only what the tested portion says. Ask what it does within the passage.

Useful function labels include:

- introduces the topic,
- presents a conventional view,
- provides an example or evidence,
- establishes a contrast,
- qualifies a previous claim,
- explains a cause or consequence,
- introduces a problem,
- offers a solution.

:::key{name="Function questions"}
Content is what a sentence says. Function is why the author included it.
:::

State the function in your own words before reading the choices. The best answer should describe both the material and its role in the author's reasoning.

## Cross-Text Connections

Summarize each passage in a short sentence, then compare the claims.

For example:

- Passage 1: “X is mainly caused by A.”
- Passage 2: “B also contributes to X.”

The relationship might be agreement, disagreement, partial agreement, a qualification, or two different perspectives on the same subject.

:::warning
Do not select an answer merely because both passages mention the same topic. It must accurately describe the relationship between their claims.
:::
