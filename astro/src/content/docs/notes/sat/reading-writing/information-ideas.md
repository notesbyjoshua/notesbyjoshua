This domain focuses on understanding what a passage states directly, what it implies, and how evidence supports its ideas.

## Central Ideas and Details

Central-idea questions ask what the passage is mostly trying to communicate. A correct answer should:

- cover most or all of the passage,
- include the central conclusion,
- avoid unnecessary details,
- match the passage's level of certainty.

:::strategy{title="Finding the central idea"}
1. Identify the topic.
2. Ask what the author says about that topic.
3. State the answer in one sentence before reading the choices.
4. Reject choices that describe only one example or piece of background information.
:::

A useful test is to complete the sentence, “The passage mainly explains that ___.” If an answer summarizes only one example, it is probably too narrow.

<div class="theorem-box">

**Example.** Biologists once assumed that urban noise only disrupted animal communication. Recent studies, however, show that some bird populations have adjusted by singing at higher pitches, allowing their calls to remain distinguishable from low-frequency traffic noise. Which choice best states the central idea of the text?

The first sentence presents the older view, while the second gives the passage's main point: some birds have adapted their songs to communicate despite urban noise. An answer discussing only traffic noise would be too broad, and an answer claiming that all birds have adapted would be too strong.

</div>

For a detail question, locate the relevant sentence and paraphrase it. Match what the passage states rather than adding an inference.

Preserve:

- who did what,
- cause and effect,
- the direction of a comparison,
- the passage's degree of certainty.

:::warning
Watch for reversals such as increase versus decrease, cause versus result, Group A versus Group B, and earlier versus later.
:::

## Inferences

An inference is one small logical step from the information in the passage, not a new theory.

:::strategy{title="Making a supported inference"}
1. Identify the facts immediately before the blank or the claim in the question.
2. Notice logical signals such as *therefore*, *however*, *for example*, or *not surprisingly*.
3. Finish the reasoning yourself.
4. Choose the answer requiring the fewest assumptions.
:::

For example, suppose a passage states that rocky planets generally cannot produce detectable ammonia without life, while mini-Neptunes can produce it through pressure and temperature alone. The supported inference is that ammonia may be a biosignature on a rocky planet but not necessarily on a mini-Neptune. The passage does not support a broader probability claim.

<div class="theorem-box">

**Example.** A museum tested two labels for the same painting. Visitors who read the shorter label spent more time looking at the painting, but both groups remembered the same number of factual details afterward. Which conclusion is best supported?

The safest inference is that the shorter label was associated with longer viewing time but not better factual recall. We cannot conclude that the label *caused* visitors to enjoy the painting more because enjoyment was not measured.

</div>

## Command of Evidence

For textual-evidence questions, break the claim into components and ask what quotation would directly prove each component.

If a claim says a species adapted because its habitat became drier, strong evidence should show:

- that the habitat became drier,
- that the species changed,
- ideally, a connection between those facts.

Evidence showing only that the species lives in a dry place is related, but insufficient.

### Graphs and Tables

Before reading the answer choices, record:

- the axes,
- the units,
- the groups being compared,
- the overall trend,
- any important numerical difference.

Then translate each answer into a numerical claim and check it against the visual.

:::mistakes
- selecting the correct trend for the wrong group or year,
- confusing a percentage with a percentage-point difference,
- claiming causation from correlation,
- using *always* when the graph shows only a general trend,
- ignoring the units.
:::

An increase from 20% to 30% is an increase of 10 percentage points, but a 50% relative increase. These descriptions are not interchangeable.

<div class="theorem-box">

**Example.** A graph shows that 35% of surveyed students biked to school in 2024, compared with 28% in 2023. State the numerical change that directly supports the conclusion that biking became more common.

The share increased by

$$
35\%-28\%=7.
$$

This is an increase of 7 percentage points. Calling it a 7% increase would confuse percentage points with relative percent change.

</div>
