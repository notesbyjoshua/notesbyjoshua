These strategies apply to both Reading and Writing and Math. Section-specific methods are covered in the individual topic notes.

## Recognizing the Question Type

Before solving, identify the question's domain and specific task. Use the wording, answer choices, and information provided as clues.

| Clue | Likely approach |
| --- | --- |
| A passage asks what is stated, implied, or supported | Locate the relevant evidence and avoid unsupported assumptions |
| A passage asks what a word, sentence, or second text does | Use context and describe the relationship or function |
| Bullet-point notes or a transition blank appears | Follow the stated rhetorical goal or identify the logical connection |
| Choices differ mainly in grammar or punctuation | Identify the exact convention being tested |
| Variables, a graph, a table, or a geometric figure appears | Name the mathematical topic and the requested quantity before calculating |

:::tip
Classifying the question is the first step of solving it. Your label should be specific enough to suggest a method, such as “linear system,” “transition—contrast,” or “central idea,” rather than simply “Math” or “Reading and Writing.”
:::

## Build Your Study Plan from Evidence

Start by identifying the topics that cost you the most points. Do not judge a topic only by whether an answer was correct: guessed answers also reveal skills that need work.

:::strategy
1. Take a practice set and record every missed or guessed question.
2. Sort each question by topic and by the reason you missed it.
3. Review the weakest skill and practice similar questions without a time limit.
4. Retake missed questions without looking at the solution.
5. Use a timed mixed set to check whether the improvement carries over.
:::

Practice one skill at a time before relying heavily on full tests:

1. Complete 10–15 questions of one type.
2. For every mistake, record the question type, why your answer was wrong, why the correct answer was right, and the rule or method you should use next time.
3. Redo missed questions about two days later without looking at the explanation.
4. Once your accuracy is consistently around 85–90%, begin adding time pressure.

Full tests are still important, but they are best used to diagnose weaknesses and check endurance. Focused sets are usually better for fixing a specific weakness.

## Keep an Error Log

Classify the cause of each miss. Useful categories include:

- content gap
- misread question
- unsupported assumption
- algebra or arithmetic error
- incorrect calculator setup
- eliminated the correct answer
- pacing or time pressure
- careless transcription

The point is to find a repeatable cause. “Careless mistake” is not useful until you identify what action produced it.

:::checklist
- What topic and question type was tested?
- At what step did my reasoning first go wrong?
- What clue, rule, or calculation did I miss?
- What should I do differently when I see this pattern again?
- Can I now solve the question without the explanation?
:::

## Manage Time by Question, Not by Pride

Do not spend the same amount of time on every question. Straightforward questions should create time for harder ones.

If you are stuck:

1. Eliminate choices or approaches you know are wrong.
2. Make your best supported choice.
3. Mark the question for review.
4. Move on and return if time remains.

One difficult question should not prevent you from reaching several questions you could solve correctly.

## Use the Answer Choices

Answer choices are information. Depending on the problem, you can:

- estimate before calculating,
- substitute choices into an equation,
- eliminate answers with the wrong sign, units, scale, or meaning,
- compare each Reading and Writing choice with the exact claim or rule being tested.

Use these methods deliberately. Randomly testing every choice can take longer than solving the problem directly.

## Review Productively

When checking a missed question, do not stop after reading the explanation. Close it and solve the question again from the beginning.

Then explain:

- why the correct method works,
- why your original method failed,
- how you will recognize the same pattern next time.

The goal is not to remember one answer. It is to build a method that transfers to a new question.

## Test-Day Reminders

- Read exactly what the question asks before solving.
- Match your answer to the requested quantity and units.
- Avoid changing a supported answer without a specific reason.
- Use remaining time to revisit marked questions and check preventable errors.
- Treat each question as a new opportunity; do not carry frustration from one problem into the next.
